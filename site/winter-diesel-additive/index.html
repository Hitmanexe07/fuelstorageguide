Winter diesel additives: what anti-gel does, when to add it, and how much

By a fuel-system technician/8 years in the trade/Updated October 28, 2026.

An anti-gel additive lets #2 diesel keep flowing through filters below its cloud point, but only if it's mixed into the fuel before the fuel gets cold. Once wax crystals have formed, a regular anti-gel can't undo them (Howes; Gold Eagle).

Most of these additives are rated to protect fuel down to around 0°F (Cenex). Colder than that, you need #1 diesel blended in. This guide covers what the additives actually do, how to treat a bulk tank, and when buying treated fuel is the smarter move.

What winter additives actually do

"Anti-gel" on the bottle usually means a cold flow improver: it changes the size and shape of wax crystals so they don't lock together on the filter. It doesn't stop wax from forming (CRC Report 671).

Ingredient typeWhat it doesWhat it can't do
Cold flow improver (the main "anti-gel")Keeps wax crystals small and needle-shaped so a porous wax layer forms on the filter instead of a solid wallBarely changes the cloud point; most lower it only 1–3°C (CRC Report 671)
Wax anti-settling additiveKeeps small wax crystals suspended so they don't settle into a waxy layer at the bottom of a cold tank (CRC Report 671)Doesn't replace a flow improver
Pour point depressantLowers the temperature where fuel stops pouring, which helps pumping in bulkOften does little for filter plugging above the pour point (CRC Report 671)
De-icerKeeps small amounts of water from freezing in filters (Power Service)Doesn't get rid of free water on the tank bottom; drain that (see water in your diesel tank)

Two things the bottle won't tell you:

  • Results depend on your fuel. Some fuels respond well to flow improvers and some barely respond, depending on how the refinery blended them (CRC Report 671). The same bottle can work great one winter and poorly the next.
  • Biodiesel blends are harder to treat. Biodiesel generally makes fuel more difficult to treat with cold flow improvers (CRC Report 671), which is why labels raise the dose for biodiesel blends (Power Service).

Treating a bulk tank

Pour the additive in right before a delivery so the incoming fuel mixes it, and do it while the fuel is still well above its cloud point.

  1. Treat early. If you don't know your fuel's cloud point, start treating when lows are forecast below about 30°F (Bell Performance; Power Service). Some fuels cloud as high as +20°F (Gold Eagle).
  2. Add it before the fuel goes in. Power Service's directions say to add the product before filling the tank (Power Service). The rush of the delivery does the mixing. An additive poured on top of a full, still tank won't spread through it well.
  3. Dose for the fuel you'll have, not the fuel you have now. Figure the dose on the gallons in the tank after the delivery. If old untreated fuel is already in the tank, the dose has to cover it too.
  4. Follow the label for cold snaps. Labels typically increase the dose below 0°F or for biodiesel blends. Power Service, for example, doubles its rate in those conditions (Power Service).
  5. Treat again with each delivery. New fuel dilutes the treatment already in the tank (EPRI).

Consider buying treated fuel instead. For a big tank, it's hard to get the additive mixed evenly yourself. EPRI's guidance for large standby tanks found that adding enough additive to treat a big storage tank and making sure it's mixed often isn't worth the cost or uncertainty compared with buying fuel already made for winter (EPRI). Many fuel suppliers deliver winterized or blended diesel; ask what cloud point and operability it's treated to.

Anti-gel, #1 blending, or heat?

Anti-gel covers most northern winters down to about 0°F. Below that, blend in #1 diesel. Heat protects the one spot that fails first: the filter.

OptionBest forLimits
Anti-gel additiveTypical winters; tanks you can treat at each deliveryTypically effective to about 0°F (Cenex); must go in before the fuel clouds
#1 diesel blendDeep cold. Cenex suggests 70/30 #2/#1 below 35°F, 30/70 deeper into winter, and straight #1 below −30°F (Cenex)Costs more per gallon; actually lowers the cloud point, unlike additives
Filter heater, heat tracing, insulationExposed dispenser filters and short runs of line, which gel before the tank does (CRC Report 671)Needs power at the tank

FAQ

Can I add anti-gel after the fuel is already cold?

It has to go in before the fuel reaches its cloud point. Once wax has formed, a preventive anti-gel won't fix it; use an emergency product like Diesel 9-1-1 (Howes; Power Service).

Can I use too much?

More isn't always better. Flow improvers give smaller gains as the dose goes up (EPRI), and some engine makers say to follow the additive maker's mixing directions exactly and not over-concentrate (NHTSA service bulletin).

Does winter diesel from my supplier already have anti-gel?

Often. Refiners and terminals blend and treat fuel to meet the cold-weather needs of the region where it's sold (CRC Report 671), and suppliers sell winterized blends with flow improvers already added (Cenex). Ask your supplier what yours has, and to what temperature it's treated.

Should I add gasoline or kerosene to keep diesel from gelling?

Never gasoline: it lowers the flash point and creates a fire hazard. Kerosene-type fuel or #1 diesel is the safe way to blend (EPRI).
