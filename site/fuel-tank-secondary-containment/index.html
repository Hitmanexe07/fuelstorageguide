Fuel tank secondary containment: sizing, dikes, and double-wall tanks

By Austin, fuel-system technician/8 years in the trade/Updated October 31, 2026.

If your site needs an SPCC plan, every bulk fuel tank needs secondary containment that holds the entire capacity of the largest tank, plus enough extra room ("freeboard") for rain. A dike, a containment basin, or a double-wall tank can all do the job. The federal rule doesn't say 110%; that's a rule of thumb.

Secondary containment is the second barrier: if the tank (the primary container) leaks or splits, the containment catches the fuel before it reaches soil or water. The requirement comes from the federal Spill Prevention, Control, and Countermeasure (SPCC) rule, so it applies once your site needs an SPCC plan. Not sure whether you do? Start with Do I need an SPCC plan?

What the rule requires

The SPCC rule has two containment requirements, and most fuel sites need both.

RequirementWhat it coversHow big
Sized containment (40 CFR 112.8(c)(2))Bulk storage tanks: your fuel tanks, used oil tanks, and other stationary containersThe entire capacity of the largest single container, plus sufficient freeboard to contain precipitation
Sized containment for mobile containers (40 CFR 112.8(c)(11))Drums, totes, and skid tanks that get moved around (mobile refuelers and tank trucks excepted)The largest single compartment or container, plus freeboard for precipitation
General containment (40 CFR 112.7(c))Everywhere else oil could spill: fill points, transfer areas, hoses and nozzles, mobile refuelersThe most likely spill, based on the typical way things fail there

The rule also says the containment, walls and floor, has to actually hold oil until cleanup: diked areas must be "sufficiently impervious to contain discharged oil" (40 CFR 112.8(c)(2)), and a spill can't escape the system before cleanup (40 CFR 112.7(c)).

"Largest single container" can be more than one tank. Tanks that are permanently manifolded together, so they work as a single storage unit, count as one container for sizing (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4).

The 110% rule and "sufficient freeboard"

The federal rule never says 110%. EPA's own inspector guidance says the rule "does not specifically define the term 'sufficient freeboard,' nor does it describe how to calculate this volume" (EPA SPCC guidance, section 4.3.2).

  • Where 110% comes from. When EPA proposed using storm data, commenters suggested 110% of tank capacity as a simpler alternative (EPA SPCC guidance, section 4.3.2). It stuck as a rule of thumb.
  • What EPA said is "proper." In the 2002 rule preamble, EPA wrote that sufficient freeboard is the amount needed to hold the rain from a 25-year, 24-hour storm (EPA SPCC guidance, section 4.3.2).
  • Who decides. The Professional Engineer certifying the plan, or the owner for a self-certified plan, sets the freeboard and should write down how it was figured (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4).
  • Indoors or under a roof. When containment is inside a building, freeboard for rain usually doesn't apply (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4).

Some fire codes use the same storm: a fire code checklist based on the International Fire Code sizes outdoor drainage for "a 24-hour rainfall as determined by a 25-year storm" (Central Arizona Fire checklist, IFC 2012).

Sizing a dike: a worked example

Rain falls on the whole dike, not just the tank, so a small dike can need more than 110%. Here's the math for a common farm setup. The storm number is only an example; look up yours in NOAA's precipitation frequency data (NOAA Atlas 14).

StepExample
The setupOne 1,000-gallon tank on a stand inside an 8 ft by 14 ft dike. The tank bottom sits above the top of the dike wall, so the tank takes up almost no room in the dike.
1. Tank volume in cubic feet1,000 gal ÷ 7.48 gal/ft³ = 133.7 ft³
2. Rain on the dike floorExample 25-year, 24-hour storm of 4.5 in = 0.375 ft. 8 × 14 = 112 ft² × 0.375 ft = 42.0 ft³ (about 314 gallons)
3. Total volume needed133.7 + 42.0 = 175.7 ft³
4. Wall height175.7 ft³ ÷ 112 ft² = 1.57 ft, about 19 inches
Compare: 110% of the tank1,100 gal = 147.1 ft³, a 15.8-inch wall. That falls about 3 inches short of the storm-based number here

Two adjustments for real sites:

  • Subtract anything sitting in the dike below the wall top: other tanks, saddles, the foundation of a tank that sits on the floor. EPA's worked example subtracts the volume the tanks displace (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4). Size the dike for the largest tank, with the other tanks counted as taking up space.
  • Leave room for the water you haven't drained yet. Rain that sits in the dike takes up the space meant for fuel.

Field note: Most of the farm and fleet setups I service use double-wall tanks rather than a single-wall tank in a dike.

Double-wall tanks

A double-wall tank can be its own secondary containment. EPA describes it as "a tank within another tank," where the inner tank stores the fuel and the outer tank is the containment (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4). Because rain can't get into the space between the walls, double-wall tanks usually don't need extra freeboard (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4).

The catch is everything outside the outer wall. EPA notes a double-wall tank doesn't contain an overfill that comes out of the vent during a delivery (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4). Piping, pumps, and dispensers outside the tank also need their own general containment (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4). EPA treats a shop-built double-wall tank as covering the general containment requirement too when it has all of these:

  • The tank was built in a shop, not in the field
  • The inner tank is a UL-listed steel tank
  • The outer tank is built to a recognized industry standard, such as API or STI
  • It has an overfill alarm, plus an automatic flow restrictor or shutoff
  • Every transfer is constantly watched

Source: EPA SPCC Guidance for Regional Inspectors, chapter 4. Without the overfill equipment, you can instead put active or passive containment where a vent overfill would land.

Inspections still apply: the rule's routine checks for leaks and deterioration cover the inner tank of a double-wall tank (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4), and double-wall tanks usually have a port for checking the space between the walls.

Field note: When I check the space between the walls, condensation is the usual find. It's common, and it has to be sponged out. Fuel in there is a different story: it usually means the inner tank has failed, which I see on tanks after years of service.

For protected tanks, fire code checklists also call for emergency venting on enclosed secondary containment (Central Arizona Fire checklist). For tank construction types, see aboveground fuel tanks.

Fill points, hoses, and fuel trailers

Containment around the tank doesn't cover where the fuel actually moves. Fill connections, the pump and hose, and areas where you fuel equipment fall under the general requirement: containment sized for the most likely spill (40 CFR 112.7(c)). Methods the rule lists include curbing, drip pans, sumps, and sorbent materials (40 CFR 112.7(c)).

Mobile refuelers and tank trucks used only on your site are excepted from sized containment (40 CFR 112.8(c)(11)), but still need general containment (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4). On farms, nurse tanks and fuel trailers count toward your SPCC capacity (EPA farm FAQ). Overfill protection is a separate SPCC requirement; the SPCC guide lists the options.

Keeping containment working

  • Drain rainwater the right way. Keep the dike's drain or bypass valve normally sealed closed, check the water for oil before you open it, reseal it afterward, and keep a record each time (40 CFR 112.8(c)(3)).
  • Inspect it. The rule calls for frequent inspection of the outside of each tank for deterioration, leaks, and "accumulation of oil inside diked areas" (40 CFR 112.8(c)(6)).
  • Keep the floor and walls tight. Seal cracks and joints in concrete, and keep earthen dikes free of burrows and erosion. The containment has to be "sufficiently impervious" (40 CFR 112.8(c)(2)).
  • Don't store junk in the dike. Anything sitting in it takes up volume you counted on for fuel.

Field note: The dike problem I run into most is rainwater that never got drained. The dike sits full, and in winter it freezes solid. A dike full of ice has no room left for a spill.

When containment isn't practical

If a dike or other containment truly can't be built, the rule allows an alternative, but it's more work, not less. The plan has to explain why containment isn't practicable, and you have to:

  • Periodically integrity test the tanks, and integrity and leak test the valves and piping
  • Have an oil spill contingency plan
  • Put a written commitment of people, equipment, and materials to clean up a spill in the plan

Source: 40 CFR 112.7(d). This route needs a Professional Engineer to certify that part of the plan, even at a self-certified site.

FAQ

Does a double-wall tank need a dike around it?

Usually not for the tank itself. The outer wall can be the containment for a leak from the inner tank (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4). You still need to deal with spills at the fill point, the hose and nozzle, and overfills through the vent.

Is 110% required?

Not by the federal rule. It requires the full capacity of the largest container plus enough freeboard for rain, and leaves the rain amount to the plan (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4). Your state, fire code, or plan certifier may set a number.

Do I need containment if I don't need an SPCC plan?

Not under the federal SPCC rule, since containment is a requirement of the plan. State rules and your fire code can still require it, and a dike or double-wall tank is cheap insurance against a cleanup bill.

Can I just drain the rainwater out of my dike?

Only if the drain valve is normally kept sealed closed, you check the water for oil first, and you keep a record each time (40 CFR 112.8(c)(3)).
