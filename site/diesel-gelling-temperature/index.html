Diesel gelling: when it happens and how to prevent it

By Austin, fuel-system technician/8 years in the trade/Updated October 28, 2026.

Untreated #2 diesel starts to cloud around 14°F and can plug a fuel filter within a few degrees of that. It doesn't need to freeze solid to stop you. Wax crystals catch on the filter long before the fuel stops pouring.

If you store diesel in a farm tank, skid tank, or transfer tank, you're at more risk than someone fueling at a truck stop. The fuel in your tank is whatever was delivered, and a load bought in September is usually summer-grade #2 with no winter treatment. This guide covers when gelling happens, why bulk tanks are different, and what to do before the first cold snap.

Cloud point, CFPP, and pour point

The number that matters for a fuel tank is the cloud point, not the "freezing" point. #2 diesel contains paraffin wax. As it cools, the wax forms crystals, and three temperatures describe how bad it gets.

StageWhat happensTypical untreated #2 diesel
Cloud pointWax crystals first appear; fuel looks hazyAbout 14°F (Cenex). Major U.S. pipelines cap winter diesel at 15°F and summer diesel at 20°F (CRC)
Cold filter plugging point (CFPP)Crystals build up fast enough to block a 45-micron test screenNear the cloud point when no additive is used (CRC)
Pour pointFuel gels and stops pouringLower still, but it says little about when equipment quits; pipelines cap winter diesel at 0°F (CRC)

Two things trip people up:

  • Your filters are finer than the test screen. CFPP is measured with a 45-micron screen. Fill-Rite's standard transfer pump filters are 10 micron, with 30- and 2-micron options (Fill-Rite), and engine fuel filters run from about 20 microns down to 2 (CRC). Finer filters can plug at a warmer temperature than the fuel's rated CFPP.
  • You can't change the cloud point with a bottle. Most anti-gel additives lower the cloud point only 1–3°C, if at all. They work by changing the size and shape of the wax crystals so fuel keeps passing through the filter (CRC). Blending in #1 diesel, which has much less wax, is what actually lowers the cloud point.

Why bulk tanks gel before vehicles do

A storage tank sits outside, holds fuel bought months ago, and pushes it through a cold filter with nothing warming it up. A running engine recirculates warm fuel; a farm tank doesn't.

  • Small tanks cool down fast. Industry guidance says tanks under 30,000 gallons can produce a lot of wax just from normal day-night temperature swings. For aboveground farm tanks, it recommends blending and additives that bring the cloud point below your expected lows (CRC).
  • The fuel may be summer grade. Fuel is blended for the season it's sold in. Diesel bought in summer or early fall won't have the cloud point of fuel made for January (EPRI). ASTM's monthly low-temperature tables are guidance, not a legal requirement, and cold performance is something you agree on with your supplier (CRC).
  • Lines and filters gel first. Hoses, pipes, and filters hold little fuel and cool faster than the tank. Fuel can gel in the line or filter while the tank is still liquid (CRC).
  • Water turns to ice. Ice can block a tank outlet or a dispenser filter, and aboveground tanks are especially vulnerable. Ice and wax plugs look the same, and sometimes it's both (CRC).
  • Wax can settle. Fuel stored below its cloud point can separate into a waxy bottom layer and a thinner top layer, so what comes out of the tank changes as the level drops (CRC).
  • Topping off changes the mix. Adding winter fuel to a half-full tank of summer fuel gives you something in between, and dilutes any additive (EPRI).

How to keep stored diesel from gelling

Treat or blend the fuel before it gets cold, and keep water out of the tank. Additives can't undo wax that has already formed, so timing matters more than which brand you buy.

  1. Order winter fuel for winter. Ask your supplier whether deliveries from late fall on are winterized or blended, and what cloud point they're treated to. If you still have summer fuel in the tank, plan to treat it.
  2. Add anti-gel while the fuel is still warm (more in winter diesel additives). Anti-gel has to be in the fuel before wax starts forming, because part of the additive works by joining the first wax crystals as they form (CRC). Additive makers say the same: add it before the fuel reaches its cloud point (Gold Eagle). The easiest way is to pour it in right before a delivery so the incoming fuel mixes it. Cenex puts the typical limit of these additives at about 0°F (Cenex).
  3. Blend in #1 diesel as it gets colder. Cenex's schedule: below 35°F, about 70% #2 and 30% #1 plus an anti-gel; deeper into winter, 30% #2 and 70% #1; below −30°F, straight #1 (Cenex).
  4. Get the water out before winter. Check the tank bottom with water-finding paste, drain or pump off any water, and change the dispenser filter in the fall. Routinely draining water bottoms is the best defense against ice (CRC). A desiccant breather on the vent cuts down on condensation.
  5. Protect the filter and short lines. Insulation and heat tracing work well on short runs of ¼″ to 1″ line, and a filter heater protects the weakest point (CRC). Never bypass or remove the filter to keep fuel moving; that sends dirt straight to your engines.
  6. Don't add gasoline. It lowers the flash point and creates a fire hazard in the tank. Use #1 diesel or a kerosene-type fuel for blending instead (EPRI).

If your fuel already gelled

Start with the filter, warm what you can safely, and don't count on a regular anti-gel to fix it. Most of the time the tank is cloudy but the filter is what's actually blocked.

  • Swap the dispenser filter first. A plugged filter is often packed with wax or ice. Replace it with a fresh one and watch whether it plugs again (CRC). Don't run without a filter.
  • Use a product made for gelled fuel. Regular anti-gel is a preventive and won't fix wax that has already formed. Emergency products such as Power Service Diesel 9-1-1 are made to reliquefy gelled fuel and de-ice frozen filters. Follow the label, then treat the fuel with an anti-gel so it doesn't gel again (Power Service).
  • Warm safely. Use a heated building, a tank or filter heater, or heat tracing. Never use an open flame or torch on a tank, pump, or fuel line.
  • Let the tank come back above the cloud point before trusting it. Wax that settled to the bottom leaves the fuel uneven until it redissolves (CRC).
  • Then fix the cause. Once it's flowing, treat or blend the fuel so it doesn't happen on the next cold night.

Winter checklist for tank owners

Do these before overnight lows reach the mid-30s:

  • Check the tank bottom for water with water-finding paste and remove any you find
  • Change the dispenser filter
  • Ask your supplier whether upcoming deliveries are winterized or blended, and to what cloud point
  • Add anti-gel to any summer fuel still in the tank, before it gets cold
  • Keep a spare filter and an emergency re-liquefier on hand
  • Check that the vent cap and fill cap seal, so snow and rain stay out
  • Plan when you'll switch to a #1 blend as temperatures drop

FAQ

At what temperature does diesel gel?

Untreated #2 diesel usually starts clouding somewhere around 10–20°F, depending on the batch; some fuels cloud as high as +20°F (Gold Eagle). Without additives, filters can plug close to that temperature, long before the fuel stops pouring.

Does #1 diesel gel?

Much later. #1 has very little wax. In one EPRI example, a #1 fuel had a cloud point of −26°F, compared with +12°F for the #2 it was blended with (EPRI).

Can I add anti-gel after the fuel has gelled?

Not regular anti-gel. It only works when mixed in above the cloud point. Use a product made for gelled fuel, and warm the filter and lines.

Does red (off-road) diesel gel differently?

The red dye marks fuel for tax purposes (EPRI). Cold behavior comes from the grade and whether the fuel was winterized or blended, so ask your supplier the same questions for off-road fuel.
