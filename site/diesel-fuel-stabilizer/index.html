Diesel fuel stabilizer: when you need it and how much to add

By Austin, fuel-system technician/8 years in the trade/Updated October 31, 2026.

A diesel stabilizer slows the oxidation that turns stored fuel dark and gummy. It only works if you add it while the fuel is fresh, because it can't reverse aging that has already happened (NREL). Use one when fuel will sit longer than you'd normally burn through it, and follow the label rate for storage.

What a stabilizer does, and what it doesn't

DoesDoesn't
Slows oxidation, the reaction that forms gums, sludge, and sediment as fuel ages (Bell Performance)Reverse aging in fuel that's already gone bad (NREL)
Helps stored fuel stay usable longer, alongside filtering and keeping water out (Alberta Agriculture)Remove water. Some products push water out of the fuel so it drops to the bottom (Gold Eagle), but you still have to drain it
Kill microbes, unless it's also an EPA-registered biocide
Prevent gelling, unless it's also an anti-gel

Diesel today has less natural protection to start with. The hydrotreating that takes sulfur out of ultra-low sulfur diesel also removes naturally occurring antioxidants, according to one fuel treatment maker (Bell Performance).

When stored diesel needs one

  • Fuel that sits for months: seasonal equipment, a farm tank filled in spring for fall, or a tank sized bigger than you use. See how long diesel lasts for typical shelf life.
  • Standby generators. That fuel sits the longest and has to work when it's needed. NFPA 110 also requires a yearly fuel quality test (Bell Performance on NFPA 110).
  • Biodiesel blends. Blends need more attention in storage, and added antioxidants extended their life in testing (NREL).

Fuel you burn within a few months usually doesn't need it if the tank is kept full, dry, and clean. A stabilizer is cheap insurance, not a substitute for keeping water out.

How to add it to a bulk tank

  1. Add it to fresh fuel, ideally right before or during a delivery, so the incoming fuel mixes it in. Antioxidants work best added when the fuel is fresh (NREL).
  2. Drain any water off the bottom first. See water in your diesel tank.
  3. Dose for the whole tank, using the tank's capacity, not just the gallons delivered, if the old fuel was never treated.
  4. Write down the date and amount so you know when it was last treated.

Biodiesel blends need the right product

Most diesel can contain up to 5% biodiesel, and higher blends like B20 age differently. One fuel treatment maker says the traditional antioxidants used in petroleum fuels, hindered phenols like BHT and TBHQ, weren't designed to fully protect biodiesel, and sells a separate biodiesel stabilizer (Bell Performance). That's a maker's claim, so treat it as a reason to check the label for biodiesel use, not as settled fact. NREL's testing found fuels with added antioxidants lasted much longer in storage (NREL).

Avoid copper, brass, and zinc in the fuel system; they speed up breakdown, especially with biodiesel blends (NREL).

FAQ

Is a stabilizer the same as anti-gel?

No. A stabilizer slows the fuel's aging in storage. Anti-gel keeps wax from plugging filters in cold weather. Some products do both, but check the label. See winter diesel additives.

Does a stabilizer kill microbes?

Not unless it's also an EPA-registered biocide, with a registration number on the label. Microbes are a separate problem with a separate fix; see diesel bugs and biocides.

Can a stabilizer bring old fuel back?

No. Antioxidants slow aging but can't reverse it (NREL). Test old fuel before you decide to keep it; see how to test diesel fuel.

Is more stabilizer better?

No. Use the label rate. STA-BIL's diesel label, for one, says "DO NOT OVERTREAT" (Gold Eagle).
