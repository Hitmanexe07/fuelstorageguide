Fuel tank vents: normal and emergency venting

By Austin, fuel-system technician/8 years in the trade/Updated October 31, 2026.

An aboveground fuel tank needs two kinds of venting. The normal vent lets the tank breathe as it's filled, emptied, and warmed or cooled. The emergency vent relieves the much higher pressure from a fire, so the tank doesn't rupture. Under the fire code, stationary aboveground tanks need emergency venting (Oregon State Fire Marshal), and the IFC farm rule calls for both (Utah County Fire Marshal).

Normal vs. emergency vents

Normal ventEmergency vent
JobLets air out as fuel goes in, and in as fuel is pumped out or the tank coolsRelieves "excessive internal pressure caused by exposure to fires" (Oregon State Fire Marshal)
What it looks likeA vent pipe with a cap, often a mushroom capA larger, spring- or weight-loaded cap that stays closed until pressure lifts it
Where it dischargesOutside, away from buildings, at a safe heightNever inside a building, for Class I, II, and III-A liquids (Oregon State Fire Marshal)

Double-wall tanks have more than one emergency vent. The spec sheet for a 300-gallon double-wall Flameshield tank, for example, lists an operating vent opening plus separate emergency vent openings for the inner and outer tanks (JME Tanks spec sheet). Fire code checklists also call for emergency venting on enclosed secondary containment (Central Arizona Fire checklist).

One exception: tanks over 12,000 gallons storing Class III-B liquids, outside the diked area or drainage path of Class I or II liquids, don't need emergency relief venting (Oregon State Fire Marshal). That doesn't cover diesel, which is normally a Class II liquid.

Fire code rules for vent placement

These come from the International Fire Code as quoted by Utah County's fire marshal and published by Oregon's (Utah County; Oregon):

  • Height: vent outlets for Class I, II, and III-A liquids release vapors at a safe point outside buildings, at least 12 feet above the adjacent ground.
  • Direction: vapors discharge upward, or horizontally away from nearby walls.
  • Clearance: at least 5 feet from building openings and from lot lines of property that can be built on, and not where eaves or other obstructions can trap vapors.
  • Piping: vent pipes drain back toward the tank with no sags or traps where liquid can collect, and are protected from damage and vibration.
  • Vent lines are only for venting unless approved, and fill openings are separate from vent openings.
  • No manifolding of vent piping except for special purposes like vapor recovery. Gasoline (Class I) tank vents aren't tied into diesel (Class II) tank vents without a way to keep gasoline vapors out, since that could change the diesel's classification.
  • Emergency vents are arranged so that if their vapors ignite, the flame doesn't heat one spot on the tank.

Farm and small-tank rules can be simpler. North Dakota's farm guidance requires normal venting on tanks under 1,100 gallons, and normal plus emergency venting over 1,100 gallons (ND farm fuel tank guide). Check your state and local fire code.

Vents and water

The vent is one of the ways water gets into a tank. Industry guidance is to verify that vents are installed and caps are sealing, and to replace a cap or repair the vent as needed (CRC Report 672). The standard monthly inspection checks that the vent cap seals against rain and blowing snow, and calls for checking vents after storms (STI SP001 checklist). A desiccant vent cap dries the air the tank breathes in (Gilbert). See water in your diesel tank.

Vents and overfills

An overfilled tank pushes fuel out the vent. EPA notes that even a double-walled tank doesn't contain an overfill that comes out of the vent during a delivery (EPA SPCC guidance, ch. 4). That's why overfill prevention matters on aboveground tanks; see secondary containment and the SPCC guide.

What to check

  • The normal vent cap is on, intact, and sheds rain and snow
  • Nothing blocks the vent: nests, ice, debris, or a cap that's been taped or plugged
  • Emergency vent caps are free to lift and aren't painted shut or weighed down
  • Vent piping is supported, undamaged, and drains back to the tank
  • A tank that whistles, sucks in, or bulges when filling or pumping points to a blocked vent

Field note: Vents do get clogged, and a clogged vent slows down deliveries. Most of the farm tanks I service have emergency vents.

FAQ

Does a small farm tank need an emergency vent?

It depends on your code. North Dakota's farm guidance requires normal venting under 1,100 gallons and both normal and emergency venting over 1,100 gallons (ND farm fuel tank guide). The IFC farm rule calls for a method of normal and emergency venting (Utah County Fire Marshal).

Can I cap off a vent to keep water out?

No. A tank needs to breathe as fuel goes in and out and as temperatures change. Keep the vent cap in good shape instead, so it sheds rain and snow.

Can two tanks share one vent pipe?

Not usually. The IFC says vent piping isn't manifolded except for special purposes like vapor recovery, and vents from gasoline tanks aren't tied into vents from diesel tanks without a way to keep gasoline vapors out (Utah County Fire Marshal, IFC references).
