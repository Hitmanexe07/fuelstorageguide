Underground tanks

UST release detection: methods, schedules, and records

By Austin, fuel-system technician/8 years in the trade/Updated October 31, 2026.

Every underground storage tank needs release detection that covers the tank and any underground piping that routinely holds product. Most tanks are monitored at least every 30 days, and pressurized piping also needs an automatic line leak detector. Since October 13, 2018, the equipment has to be tested for proper operation at least once a year (40 CFR 280.40; 280.41).

Whatever method you use has to be installed and calibrated to the manufacturer's instructions and meet the performance standards in the rule (40 CFR 280.40(a)). A system that can't use any method that complies has to be closed (40 CFR 280.40(c)).

Piping

PipingInstalled on or before April 11, 2016Installed or replaced after April 11, 2016
PressurizedAutomatic line leak detector, plus an annual line tightness test or monthly monitoringInterstitial monitoring every 30 days, plus an automatic line leak detector
SuctionLine tightness test every 3 years, or monthly monitoringInterstitial monitoring every 30 days

Source: 40 CFR 280.41(b).

  • Line leak detectors have to catch a 3 gallon per hour leak at 10 psi within an hour, and line tightness tests have to detect 0.1 gallon per hour at 1.5 times operating pressure (40 CFR 280.44). See line leak detectors.
  • "Safe suction" piping needs no release detection if all of these are true: it runs below atmospheric pressure, slopes back to the tank so it drains if suction is lost, has only one check valve per line, located directly below and as close as practical to the pump, and there's a way to verify all of that (40 CFR 280.41(b)).

The yearly equipment test

Electronic and mechanical release detection components have to be tested for proper operation at least once a year, following the manufacturer's instructions, a code of practice such as PEI RP1200, or your state's requirements (40 CFR 280.40(a)(3)). As applicable, the test covers:

  • Automatic tank gauge and other controllers: test the alarm, verify the system configuration, and test the battery backup
  • Probes and sensors: inspect for buildup, make sure floats move freely, check that the shaft isn't damaged and cables are free of kinks and breaks, and test alarms and communication with the controller
  • Automatic line leak detectors: simulate a leak to show it meets the 3 gallon per hour standard
  • Vacuum pumps and pressure gauges: make sure they communicate with sensors and the controller
  • Hand-held electronic sampling equipment for groundwater and vapor monitoring: make sure it works

Field note: On yearly tests I find failures in every one of these: sensors, floats, backup batteries, and line leak detectors.

Between yearly tests, the 30-day walkthrough checks that release detection is running with no alarms and that records are current. See the walkthrough checklist.

When release detection alarms

Report a suspected release to your state within 24 hours, unless your state sets another period (40 CFR 280.50). That includes monitoring results or an alarm that indicate a release may have occurred. You don't have to report if you find the monitoring device was defective, fix or replace it right away, and further monitoring doesn't confirm the first result (40 CFR 280.50(c)).

Unusual operating conditions also count, such as erratic dispensing, a sudden loss of product, unexplained water in the tank, or liquid between the walls of a double-walled system, unless you find the equipment isn't releasing and fix it immediately (40 CFR 280.50(b)).

Field note: I do see alarms get silenced instead of investigated. Silencing an alarm doesn't clear it; find out what triggered it.

FAQ

Is an automatic tank gauge enough by itself?

It can be, for the tank. The gauge has to detect a 0.2 gallon per hour leak at least every 30 days, and you also have to meet the monthly inventory control requirements (40 CFR 280.43(d)). Tanks installed after April 11, 2016 have to use interstitial monitoring instead (40 CFR 280.41). Piping needs its own release detection.

What counts as a suspected release?

Spilled product at the site, unusual operating conditions like erratic dispensing, a sudden loss of product, unexplained water in the tank, or liquid between the walls, and monitoring results or alarms that indicate a release (40 CFR 280.50). Report within 24 hours unless your state sets another period, or unless you find a defective part and fix it right away.

Do farm tanks need release detection?

Not if they're 1,100 gallons or less and hold motor fuel for noncommercial use; those aren't USTs under the federal rule (40 CFR 280.12). Your state may still regulate them.
